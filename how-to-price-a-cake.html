How to price a cake

A cake's price starts from what it costs you to make. Work that out in four parts, and the price follows from one division.

The figures on this page are an example. Yours will differ, and the price you charge is yours to decide.

Cost the ingredients

Go through the recipe line by line, with the filling and frosting included. For each line, work out what share of the bag, block or carton you use, and take that share of what you paid. A cup of all-purpose flour from a 5 lb bag at $5.49 costs about 29 cents. How to cost a recipe from bag prices.

Keep the small lines. Vanilla, food coloring and sprinkles are cents a cake, and they add up over a year of cakes.

Count your time

Count every minute the cake needs from you: baking, leveling, filling, the crumb coat, decorating, boxing, the messages with the customer and the washing up. Multiply by an hourly rate you choose. On a decorated cake this is often the biggest line. What time to count.

Add packaging

The box, the cake board, dowels for a tiered cake, and anything else that leaves with it. Count each at what it cost you.

Add overhead

The oven's power, pans and tools that wear out, card reader fees. These belong to no one cake, so many bakers add them as a percentage of ingredients and labor. How to work out the percentage.

Set the price at a margin

Add the four parts to get what the cake costs. Then choose a margin: the share of the price left once the cost is covered. The price is the cost divided by (1 − margin). At a 30% margin, divide by 0.70.

Adding 30% to the cost gives a lower price, and a margin of only about 23%. Markup and margin explained.

A worked example

An 8-inch two-layer vanilla cake with buttercream, serving 16.

Ingredients$18.40

Labor
3 h 30 min at $20.00 an hour$70.00

Packaging
Box and cake board$4.25

Overhead
10% of ingredients and labor$8.84

Cost of the cake$101.49

Price at 30% margin$144.99

About $9.07 a serving.

$101.49 ÷ 0.70 = $144.99, rounded up to the cent. Divided by 16 servings, that's about $9.07 each.

Check it a serving at a time

Customers often compare cakes by the serving. A tiered or carved cake takes far more time a serving than a sheet cake, and the labor line is where that shows.

When the price looks high

The arithmetic doesn't set the price. If the figure is more than your customers will pay, the parts show where to look: a design that takes less time, a smaller size, or a simpler box. A lower margin is a choice too, made knowing what it leaves you.
