Counting your time

The flour and butter are on a receipt. Your time isn't, so it's the cost most often left out of a price. On a decorated cake it's often the biggest line.

The arithmetic

Labor = minutes ÷ 60 × hourly rate.

Four dozen cookies, timed step by step, at $18.00 an hour:

Mixing and shaping35 min

Baking, four trays60 min

Cooling and packing25 min

Washing up15 min

Your time135 min

Labor at $18.00 an hour$40.50

135 ÷ 60 × $18.00 = $40.50.

What time to count

  • Measuring, mixing and shaping
  • Baking time you spend watching, turning or swapping trays
  • Cooling, leveling, filling and decorating
  • Packing, boxing and handing over or delivering
  • Shopping for the order
  • Messages with the customer, and writing the quote
  • Washing up and cleaning down

Time you're free to do something else, like dough proofing overnight, is yours to count or not. Many bakers leave it out and count the steps that need their hands.

Time it once

Guesses run short. Next time you bake the recipe, note the clock at each step, and write the total on the recipe. It changes little from bake to bake, so once is usually enough.

Choosing an hourly rate

The rate is yours to decide. One way to think about it: what would you want to be paid an hour to do this work for someone else? Use the same rate on every recipe so the prices compare fairly.

Spreading time across a batch

Time belongs to the batch, not the piece. The $40.50 above spreads across 48 cookies at about 84 cents each. Doubling a batch rarely doubles the time, which is why a bigger batch often costs less a piece.
