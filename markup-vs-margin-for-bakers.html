Markup and margin

Both describe the gap between what a bake costs you and what you charge. They measure that gap against different things, so the same gap gives two different percentages.

Markup is measured against the cost

Markup = (price − cost) ÷ cost.

A cake that costs $20 and sells for $30 has a $10 gap. $10 ÷ $20 is a 50% markup.

Margin is measured against the price

Margin = (price − cost) ÷ price.

The same cake: $10 ÷ $30 is a 33.3% margin. On any bake with a gap, the margin is the smaller of the two percentages.

Pricing from a margin

To reach a margin, divide the cost: price = cost ÷ (1 − margin). For a 50% margin on a $20 cake, $20 ÷ 0.50 = $40.

The common slip is adding the margin percentage to the cost. Adding 50% to $20 gives $30, which is a 33.3% margin, a third less than meant.

Converting one to the other

  • Margin = markup ÷ (1 + markup)
  • Markup = margin ÷ (1 − margin)

A 100% markup, doubling the cost, is a 50% margin. Use whichever you like, as long as you know which one you mean.

Why a margin can't reach 100%

A 100% margin would mean the bake cost nothing. As the margin climbs toward it, the price climbs without limit: at 90% the price is ten times the cost, and at 95% twenty times. Dough Math and the calculator on this site stop at 94.9%.

Start from the whole cost

Either percentage is only as good as the cost under it. If the cost leaves out your time or the box, the margin looks bigger than it is. Count your time, overhead and packaging as well as the ingredients.
