How the price is worked out

  • Labor = minutes ÷ 60 × hourly rate
  • Overhead = (ingredients + labor) × overhead %
  • Batch cost = ingredients + labor + packaging + overhead
  • Price = batch cost ÷ (1 − margin)

Each part rounds to the nearest cent, and the batch cost is the sum of the rounded parts. The price rounds up to the cent, so it never falls short of the margin you asked for. Overhead is taken on ingredients and labor only, because packaging is already counted at what it cost.

The example works like this. Labor is 90 ÷ 60 × $20.00 = $30.00. Overhead is ($14.20 + $30.00) × 10% = $4.42. The batch cost is $14.20 + $30.00 + $3.50 + $4.42 = $52.12. At a 30% margin the price is $52.12 ÷ 0.70 = $74.46, or $6.21 a slice when the cake serves 12.

Why the margin divides

A 30% margin means 30% of the price is left once the cost is covered, so the cost is the other 70%. Dividing by 0.70 gets you there. Adding 30% to the cost gives $67.76 instead, which is a margin of about 23%. Markup and margin, side by side.

The closer the margin gets to 100%, the faster the price climbs, and at 100% no price works. So a margin of 95% or more is worked out at 94.9%.

Filling it in

The ingredients figure is the one to get right first. Cost a recipe from the bags you buy, and use grams per cup when the recipe is in cups. For the rest, read counting your time and overhead and packaging.

The calculator does arithmetic. It can't tell you what customers near you will pay, and the price you charge is yours to decide.

What this page does with your data

Nothing. The figures are worked out in your browser. The page sends nothing, stores nothing, sets no cookies and needs no account. Close it and the figures are gone.

Where Dough Math fits

This calculator needs you to know what the ingredients cost. Dough Math works that out: enter each pantry item once, the way you bought it, write the recipe in cups or grams, and it shows the batch cost and a suggested price at the margin you choose.

Labor minutes at your hourly rate, packaging and overhead count toward the cost, the same way they do here. When butter goes up, change it once and every recipe that uses it shows the new cost.
